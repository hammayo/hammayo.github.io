The merge had been started. Two of the three participants had done their part and reported back. The third, the one that moved addresses, had said nothing. Not a success and not a failure, just silence.

The orchestrator did what it was built to do: it waited. An hour later it was still waiting. The operator's screen said "in progress" and would go on saying it, because nothing had told the orchestrator that anything was wrong.

[Part 8](/blogs/services-in-practice-part-8-orchestration-or-choreography-merging-duplicate-records/) argued that a merge across services needs one owner. This part looks at the same flow from the other side, a participant's, and at the rule that stops it hanging: **in a saga, every participant answers**.

## The failure that makes no noise

A saga is a long-running process made of local steps across services. It works only if the orchestrator learns the outcome of every step.

A participant can fail in three ways. It can succeed and say so. It can fail and say so. Or it can fail and say nothing: a handler that throws before it replies, a message that is discarded, a bug that logs an error and carries on.

The third is the dangerous one, because the orchestrator can't distinguish it from a slow success. Nothing arrives, so it waits. The whole saga stalls on one participant's silence.

## The rule: always reply

The fix is a discipline, not a clever mechanism. Whatever happens inside a participant, it sends a reply. A step that worked replies "done". A step that couldn't be completed replies "failed", with a reason.

```csharp
public async Task HandleAsync(MoveAddressesCommand command, CancellationToken ct)
{
    try
    {
        await _addresses.MoveAsync(command.FromCustomerId, command.ToCustomerId, ct);
        await _replies.SendAsync(new StepResult(command.ProcessId, "addresses", StepStatus.Done), ct);
    }
    catch (Exception ex)
    {
        _logger.LogError(ex, "Address move failed for process {ProcessId}", command.ProcessId);
        await _replies.SendAsync(
            new StepResult(command.ProcessId, "addresses", StepStatus.Failed, ex.Message), ct);
    }
}
```

"I failed" is a perfectly good answer. It's information. It lets the orchestrator mark the saga as needing attention, notify someone and stop waiting. Silence gives it nothing to act on.

The same reasoning applies to the cases where a participant decides it has nothing to do. If the customer has no addresses to move, the answer is "done, nothing to move", not no reply at all.

## Register, then proceed

The start of the saga has a smaller version of the same problem. The orchestrator needs to know which participants will take part, and whether they're ready.

In the approach we used, a participant registers its interest first, and the orchestrator then proceeds to issue the real work. Registration says "I'm here and I know about this process".

I'd note, as an honest observation, that registration alone doesn't prove readiness. A participant can register and then be unable to do its part. A stronger design would make the first phase a genuine readiness check: can you do this, right now, for these records? That would catch problems before any step has changed anything.

It matters because the cheapest failure is the one that happens before any data has moved.

## Make each step idempotent

Messages get delivered more than once, and orchestrators resend commands when they're unsure. Every participant's step has to cope.

Moving addresses twice must give the same result as moving them once. That's the same idea as the idempotent consumers earlier in the Messages That Survive series, applied to a command: key the work by the process ID and the step, and make the second arrival a no-op that sends the same reply again.

Replaying the reply matters. If the first reply was lost, the orchestrator will ask again, and the participant should answer again with the same result.

## Audit each move

Merging two people's records is a significant change. Whoever looks at it later will want to know what moved, when, and on whose authority.

Every participant records its own part: the process ID, the records involved, the before and after, and the time. The orchestrator records the sequence. Together, those two give a complete account without anyone having to reconstruct it from several logs.

This is also what makes "I failed" useful to a person. A failure with a reason, tied to a process ID, is something an operator can act on.

## Participants in configuration

Which services take part in a merge is a decision that changes over time. A new service that holds customer data needs to be included without redeploying the orchestrator.

Hold the list of participants in configuration, not in code. Adding one becomes a deliberate, reviewable change, and the orchestrator can compare the replies it received with the list of those it expected. An expected participant that never answered is, again, something it can notice.

## What compensation you still need

Replying doesn't remove the need to undo. If the order step succeeded and the payment step failed, the saga is in a state that nobody wants, and the orchestrator has to decide what to do.

For some steps there's a clean reversal: move the orders back. For others there's only compensation: add a note, raise a task for a person. It helps to decide for each step, in advance, whether it can be undone and how.

Without that thinking, "I failed" gets you an accurate picture of a mess. With it, you get a path out.

## Where it falls over

Always replying isn't free. Every participant now needs a code path for failure that sends a message, and that code can itself fail. If the reply is lost, the orchestrator is back to waiting, so it still needs a timeout and a way to ask again.

A reply also describes what the participant believes happened. A bug that reports "done" when the work was wrong defeats the whole scheme. The answer is only as good as the step behind it.

And a saga with many participants is a lot to hold in one's head. If you find yourself adding steps to get one flow right, it may be a sign that a boundary is in the wrong place.

## The takeaway

**A participant that fails silently leaves the whole saga waiting; always reply, even with "I failed".** Make each step idempotent and audited, hold participants in configuration, prefer a real readiness check in the first phase, and decide in advance how each step is undone.

Look at one multi-service flow you run and ask what happens if a single participant says nothing at all. If the answer is "it waits forever", you know what to add.
